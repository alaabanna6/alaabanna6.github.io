At a glance

The problem

Monstrum runs twelve AI agents that research, write, optimize and publish content across SEO, AEO and GEO. Its clients are marketing managers who want one answer. Is my brand showing up in AI search?

My role

I was the only designer. I defined the product from scratch, from competitor research and information architecture to the core metric and a full high fidelity web app.

The result

A complete high fidelity design, now in development. It covers sign up, onboarding, new and returning clients, the nine step pipeline, content review and performance, refined through several rounds of founder review.

01 · The challenge

A technical engine, built for a non technical client

Monstrum's engine is complex by design. Twelve agents run in a continuous loop, and performance data feeds back into strategy on its own. The person paying for it is a marketing manager with a few minutes a day and no interest in how agents work.

There was also nothing to copy. I spent two weeks studying the market before drawing a screen, and each competitor solved only one piece.

The gap

No product ran SEO, AEO and GEO as one compounding pipeline. With no interface to benchmark against, the structure, the core metric and the interaction model all had to be designed from first principles.

02 · Key decisions

Four decisions that shaped the product

01

Twelve agents became nine plain steps

I grouped the agents into nine steps a client can follow, each with one of three states. A step is complete, running or locked. Clients see progress, never machinery.

Ruled out

Showing every agent and its status. It was accurate, but it turned a simple question into a systems diagram.

02

One number answers the first question

The dashboard opens with the AI Citation Score, a single measure of how often AI engines cite the brand. Within ten seconds a client can tell whether they are showing up, what changed, and what to do next.

Ruled out

Leading with traffic and ranking tables. They describe classic search well and say nothing about AI answers.

03

System language became human language

Early tables told clients which agent owned each piece, such as Agent 8 or Agent 5. The founder pointed out that those numbers meant nothing to a marketing manager. Every agent reference left the interface. The Activity column now reads Monitoring, Needs review or Writing.

Ruled out

Keeping agent names with a tooltip to explain them. If a label needs explaining, it is the wrong label.

04

Day one always shows the next step

Onboarding asks for one thing, a website address. The engine reads the site and shows what it learned as editable tags to confirm. A new client then lands on a dashboard where a single banner and a single active step point to the same first action.

Ruled out

A dashboard full of zeros. It was honest, but it told a new client nothing about where to begin.

03 · Iteration

From wireframe to product

The founder reviewed the wireframes in detail over several rounds. Three changes mattered most. Each one is shown as the first wireframe next to the final design.

The pipeline moved beside the content

It began as a row of dots at the bottom of the dashboard, where it read as an afterthought. It became its own column, with a name and a status on every step.

Findings became editable

The first version told clients every field was editable but gave them no visible way to do it. In the final design every tag carries its own remove control, so fixing a wrong detail takes one click.

Onboarding fits any business

Labels like Copy tone, Team info and Testimonials did not suit every client. A law firm has no testimonials in the way a software company does. Every step became general, and the final design adds a progress count and a live result under each one.

04 · The product

Every surface, for new and returning clients

The prototype is built on my own design system and covers two journeys in one flow. A new client goes from sign up through onboarding to a first pipeline step. A returning client lands on live data, reviews content and tracks performance.

A pipeline that shows its work

Running a step updates the progress bar, the scores and the activity feed, so the client always knows what the engine is doing.

Review before anything goes live

Each draft opens with an optimization score and a short checklist. The client approves it or asks for changes.

One library, filtered by status

Tabs follow the life of a piece, from planned and drafting through review to published.

Performance that ends in an action

Every chart leads to a next step. A drop in citations comes with a suggested fix and a button to schedule it.

Brand memory the client can correct

Everything the engine knows about the brand sits on one page, and every field can be edited.

A calendar the pipeline fills in

The publishing plan is generated by the engine, with colour showing where each piece stands.

Onboarding in one field

A website address is all it takes to start. A side rail shows the four steps ahead.

05 · Process

Where AI helped, and where it didn't

Every high fidelity screen is my own work in Figma. AI sped up the process around the design. It helped me visualise ideas faster, so I could compare directions early and spend my time on the decisions.

AI sped up

  • Visualising early ideas quickly, so I could compare directions before committing.
  • Rough first layouts to react to, before I designed the real screens.
  • Checking finished screens for overlap, overflow and inconsistent labels.

I designed and decided

  • Every high fidelity screen, component and state.
  • The nine step model, the citation score and the product structure.
  • The language clients read on every screen.
  • What changed after each round of founder feedback, and what was rebuilt when it missed the bar.

06 · Outcome

With no reference product to learn from, the business model became the brief.

Every strong decision on this project, from the nine step pipeline to the plain language labels, came from understanding how Monstrum works and who pays for it before drawing a single screen.

After seven months the design was complete. I met with the developers to hand it over, and Monstrum is now in development.
